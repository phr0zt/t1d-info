This page is for the therapist. A client with type 1 diabetes doesn't need a different massage — they need a slightly longer intake, a few adjustments around their devices and insulin sites, and someone in the room who knows what a low looks like.

## Intake

Ask, every visit until you know the client:

- **Pump or injections**, and where they last injected or where the pump site is
- **Glucose now, and its direction** — steady, rising or falling
- **Any lows in the last day**, especially one that needed help
- **Numb areas**, wounds or slow-healing sores, dizziness when standing up
- Stiff joints, recent eye surgery, and anything else on your usual health history

The FQM suggests the client check glucose before and after at least the first two or three sessions.

## On the table

- **Devices.** Keep a hand's width of clear skin around the CGM sensor and the pump site. No oil there, no pressure, no stretching across them.
- **Insulin sites.** Skip recent injection sites and the pump site, and no heat over them. Massage and heat both make insulin act faster — FIT Canada advises against massaging an injection site right after a dose.
- **Numb skin.** Broad, full-hand pressure rather than deep point work. No hot stones or heat packs on areas the client can't feel.
- **Sugar.** Make sure the client's fast sugar is within *their* reach, not in their bag.
- **Getting up.** Ask them to sit on the edge of the table before standing.

If the sensor alarms during the session, that's not an interruption to apologise for. Give the client a moment to read it. A sudden low reading while they are lying on the sensor may be a compression low — but let them confirm it with a finger prick rather than assuming.

## Spot a low

Sweating, trembling, sudden restlessness, confusion, slurred or unusual speech, or simply going quiet. Lying relaxed with eyes closed, these signs are easy to miss.

:::emergency[Sugar first, questions after]
- Stop the session. Help them sit up slowly.
- Fast sugar — 15 g — then recheck in 15 minutes.
- Unconscious or having a seizure: call 911. Nothing by mouth.
:::

## Things worth noticing

- **Lumps at injection sites.** Rubbery lumps (lipohypertrophy) are common. Insulin acts unpredictably there; a gentle "you might mention that to your diabetes team" is genuinely useful.
- **Stiff shoulders and hands.** Frozen shoulder and stiff hands are common after many years with type 1. If a shoulder has been stiffening for weeks, suggest a diagnosis from a doctor or physiotherapist alongside your work.
- **Reasons to reschedule.** Raised ketones, a severe low in the last 24 hours, fever or acute illness, or wounds and infection in the treatment area.

Most clients with type 1 have explained their diabetes many times. A therapist who asks the right questions first, without fuss, is one they come back to.
