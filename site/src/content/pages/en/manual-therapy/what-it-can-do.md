A lot of what circulates online about massage and diabetes promises more than the research shows. This page sorts out who the different hands-on therapists are in Québec, why people with type 1 often end up seeing one, and which benefits actually hold up.

## Four titles on the clinic door

**Massage therapist (massothérapeute).** Therapeutic massage. Massage therapy is **not regulated** in Québec: there is no professional order, and the title isn't protected. Standards come from the association the therapist belongs to. Ontario and British Columbia regulate massage therapists; Québec does not.

**Orthotherapist (orthothérapeute).** Massage combined with movement therapy, aimed at muscle pain and joint stiffness. It is a specialisation taken after massage training — roughly 1,000 to 1,300 hours in all, depending on the school. Orthotherapy is **not regulated** either.

**Kinesiologist (kinésiologue).** Exercise as treatment, represented by a federation (FKQ). Québec announced in June 2026 that kinesiology will join a professional order, but that is not in force yet.

**Physiotherapist (physiothérapeute).** **Regulated** by the Ordre professionnel de la physiothérapie du Québec (OPPQ), which keeps a public directory. The first call for an injury, a frozen shoulder, or anything that needs a diagnosis.

:::note
In Québec, a *kinésithérapeute* is an unregulated movement therapist. It is not the same as the French *masseur-kinésithérapeute*, which in France is the regulated physiotherapy equivalent. People who have lived in France often mix the two up.
:::

## Why people with type 1 end up on the table

Hand and shoulder problems are common after many years with type 1, and they are often mistaken for ordinary ageing.

:::stats
- **66%** of 1,217 adults in the DCCT/EDIC study — on average 31 years with type 1 — had at least one hand or shoulder condition
- **31%** frozen shoulder: a painful shoulder that slowly stiffens
- **30%** carpal tunnel: numb, tingling fingers, often at night
- **28%** trigger finger: a finger that catches or locks when it bends
- **22%** stiff hands — unable to press the palms flat together (the "prayer sign")
:::

Dupuytren's contracture affected 9%. Frozen shoulder is about five times more likely with diabetes than without.

These figures come from people who were diagnosed decades ago, and how common these conditions are with today's care isn't known yet. The leading explanation is that glucose slowly stiffens collagen over years (glycation) — a matter of time and exposure, not a verdict on effort. In the original trial, people who had received intensive treatment and those who had received conventional treatment had similar rates: 64% versus 68%, not a significant difference.

## What massage can honestly claim

**Reasonably supported.** Feeling calmer. A meta-analysis of 37 trials found that a single massage session lowered anxiety, blood pressure and heart rate. For frozen shoulder in people with diabetes, joint mobilisation combined with exercise had the largest effect across 8 trials — although the evidence is low quality.

**Disputed.** "Massage lowers stress hormones." One review reported cortisol falling by about a third after massage. A stricter meta-analysis, comparing massage groups with control groups, found the effect on cortisol indistinguishable from zero. And stress itself moves blood sugar differently in different people with type 1: up for some, down for others, and not at all for some.

**Not shown.** Treating diabetes. A few small studies — mostly in children, mostly without a proper comparison group — saw glucose fall after massage sessions. The most likely reason is that massage speeds up the absorption of insulin already injected. That is a reason to check your glucose, not a therapy. There is also no good evidence that massage prevents or treats complications by "improving circulation", and no diabetes guideline lists it.

:::note[Evidence grade]
Every claim about massage and blood sugar rests on low-quality evidence: small, short studies, most of them without a control group, several using older insulins.
:::

So go because it feels good, eases tension and helps you move — not to treat diabetes. That is a perfectly good reason to book.
