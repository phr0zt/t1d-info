Complications don't rule out massage. People with numb feet or stiff shoulders often benefit from hands-on care, at very little risk, as long as the therapist knows what to adjust. This page covers the adjustments.

## If your feet or hands have lost feeling

Neuropathy removes the one safety signal massage relies on: you saying "that's too much". So the therapist has to judge for you.

- **Broad pressure.** On numb areas, ask for broad, full-hand pressure rather than deep point work or aggressive stretching.
- **Look afterwards.** Do the same foot check you already do daily again after the session: redness, blisters, broken skin.
- **Draw the map.** Tell the therapist where the numbness starts and stops. That is more useful than the word "neuropathy".

:::warning[No heat on numb skin]
Hot stones, heat packs and hot towels can burn skin that can't feel them. Diabetes Canada already advises testing bath water by hand for the same reason.
:::

There is a small signal of benefit, too. In a trial of 60 people with type 2 diabetes and neuropathy, two weeks of Thai foot massage improved balance and sensation tests. It was small, short and in type 2, so treat it as encouraging rather than proven.

## Stiff shoulders and hands: who to see

Frozen shoulder, carpal tunnel, trigger finger and stiff hands are all common after many years with type 1 (see [who does what](/en/manual-therapy/what-it-can-do/)). Orthotherapists often see them first, because people book for "a stiff shoulder".

**Get a diagnosis first.** A shoulder that stiffens over weeks, a finger that catches, numb fingers at night: see your doctor or a physiotherapist, not only a massage therapist.

**What has the most support.** Joint mobilisation combined with exercise, from a physiotherapist or a trained therapist, had the largest effect in a review of eight trials in people with diabetes. The evidence is low quality, and recovery tends to be slower with diabetes — expect it to take a while.

**Where massage fits.** Alongside, for muscle tension and comfort. Gentle range-of-motion work matters more than force.

**If you are offered a steroid injection.** A cortisone injection into a shoulder or hand can raise blood sugar for a while afterwards. Plan the days that follow with your diabetes team — see [medicines that raise blood sugar](/en/medications/medicines-that-raise-blood-sugar/).

## Lumps, skin and getting up

**Lumps where insulin goes.** Rubbery lumps under the skin at injection or pump sites — lipohypertrophy — affect about a third of people with type 1, in pooled studies. A therapist may notice them before you do. Insulin given into those spots acts unpredictably, so mention them to your team. It is a site-rotation issue, not a personal failing.

**Skin.** Avoid any wound, sore, blister or infection in the treatment area. Moisturising dry feet is fine — just not between the toes.

**Getting up.** Some people who have lived with type 1 for a long time feel light-headed when they stand up. Sit on the edge of the table for a moment, then stand slowly.

**Eyes.** If you've had recent eye surgery or have advanced retinopathy, ask your eye specialist about time spent face-down.
