This page explains the one rule everyone around a person with type 1 should know, why the kind of sugar matters, and the cases where 15 grams is the wrong amount.

## The 15-15 rule

Diabetes Canada and Diabète Québec give the same four steps for a low in an adult. Diabetes Canada's 2018 guideline sets the line at 4.0 mmol/L. Diabète Québec and most CGM reports use 3.9. The advice is the same either way.

:::emergency[Under 4.0 mmol/L]
1. Take **15 g** of fast-acting carbohydrate — ideally glucose or table sugar, as tablets or dissolved in water.
2. Sit down and wait **15 minutes**. Don't keep eating while you wait.
3. Check again. Still under 4.0? Take another 15 g.
4. Once you're back up, eat the meal that was due — or, if it's more than an hour away, a snack with 15 g of carbohydrate and some protein.
:::

## Why some sugar works faster

On average, 15 g of glucose raises blood sugar by about **2.1 mmol/L within 20 minutes**. 20 g raises it by about 3.6 mmol/L at 45 minutes. The kind of sugar decides how fast that happens:

- **Glucose is fastest.** Glucose tablets such as Dex4, and Rockets candy, are pure glucose (dextrose). It doesn't need digesting.
- **Table sugar is close behind.** Sucrose is half glucose. Sugar dissolved in water and regular pop both work well.
- **Fructose is slower.** Fruit juice and honey contain a lot of fructose, which the body has to convert first. Diabetes Canada says glucose or sucrose tablets or solution are preferable to orange juice. ISPAD found fructose-based treatment less effective.
- **Fat and protein are slowest.** They hold sugar in the stomach. That is why chocolate, milk and ice cream aren't first choices.
- **Glucose gel is surprisingly slow.** It gives less than 1.0 mmol/L at 20 minutes.

Juice still works, and it's fine when it's what you have. It just isn't the fastest thing in the fridge.

## Not everyone needs 15 g

### Children

Diabetes Canada sets children's amounts by age:

| Age | Carbohydrate | Glucose tablets (4 g) | Juice or regular pop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 5 | 5 g | 1 | 40 mL |
| 5 to 10 | 10 g | 2 or 3 | 85 mL |
| Over 10 | 15 g | 4 | 125 mL |

ISPAD, the international pediatric society, uses body weight instead: 0.3 g per kilogram, which works out to about 9 g at 30 kg and 15 g above 50 kg. The two methods land in similar places. Your child's team will tell you which one they use.

### Someone who needs help but can still swallow

For an adult, Diabetes Canada recommends **20 g**. Diabète Québec's list for 20 g is 5 Dex4 tablets, 200 mL (¾ cup) of regular pop or juice, or 3 rolls of Rockets. If they can't swallow safely, give nothing by mouth. That is when to use [glucagon](/en/emergencies/glucagon/) and call 911.

### Automated insulin delivery

Closed-loop pumps (Omnipod 5, Tandem Control-IQ, Medtronic 780G and others) often cut or pause insulin before a low arrives. A full 15 g on top of that can push blood sugar high afterwards.

:::note[Where the guidance differs]
ISPAD 2022 suggests considering a smaller treatment — **5 to 10 g** — on automated systems. That rests on expert opinion (grade E), not trials. The international consensus on automated insulin delivery and Insulet's Omnipod 5 guidance also say many people need less, without giving a number. Diabetes Canada's 2018 guideline predates these systems and doesn't address it. Agree on your amount with your team.
:::

### Many people need more

A Canadian study found that people used an average of 32 g to treat a low. Start at the standard amount, check again, and set your own number with your team.
