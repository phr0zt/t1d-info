The hardest part of treating a low usually isn't knowing what to take. It's stopping at the right amount. This page covers the "medicine, not food" idea, why your sensor can make you overtreat, and how to keep supplies where you need them.

## A low makes you ravenous

Low blood sugar triggers intense hunger. That's biology, not weak willpower. Add the shaking, the fog and the 15-minute wait, and eating far more than 15 g becomes the most common mistake there is. It is also completely understandable. The result is often a high a couple of hours later, and sometimes a cycle of chasing numbers up and down.

## Pick a "medicine" candy

The best idea in Beyond Type 1's chart is to treat your low supplies as medicine, not food.

:::do[Make the dose easy to get right]
- **Choose something you don't snack on for fun.** Tablets, Rockets, a specific candy you don't love. Boring is good.
- **Pre-portion it.** Count 15 g into small bags or tins ahead of time, so you can't take a handful in the middle of a low.
- **Write the amount on it.** "2 rolls" or "7 pieces" in marker. At 3.1 mmol/L, nobody does division.
:::

Thinking of it as medicine also takes the shame out of it. You aren't sneaking candy; you are taking a measured dose.

## Trust the clock, not the sensor

A CGM measures sugar in the fluid under the skin, which trails blood sugar. The American Diabetes Association notes that sensors can lag a meter by **5 to 15 minutes**. After you treat a low, the sensor may still show a low number when your blood sugar has already started to rise.

ISPAD advises against treating a second time just because the sensor still reads low at 15 minutes. Confirm with a finger prick first. Diabète Québec says the same about any reading that doesn't match how you feel.

## A rebound high is not a failure

If you overtreat and go high, that's a number to correct, not a judgement. It happens to everyone with type 1. Next time, the pre-portioned bag makes it easier.

## Where to keep it

- **On you:** a pocket, purse or phone case. A roll of Rockets or 4 tablets weighs almost nothing.
- **Bedside, car and gym bag:** these are the lows you can't walk to the kitchen for. In a hot car gummies melt, and tablets cope better.
- **School, daycare and work:** a labelled kit with the amount written on it, so anyone can hand it over without asking. Your team or the school nurse can help with a school plan.
- **Check monthly:** restock what got eaten and check expiry dates. Tablets crumble in damp places, and juice boxes can burst in a freezing car.

## For the people around them

:::emergency[Sugar first, questions later]
If someone with type 1 seems confused, sweaty, irritable or strangely quiet, offer regular pop, glucose tablets or Rockets straight away, and ask questions afterwards. If they can't swallow safely, are unconscious or are having a seizure, give nothing by mouth: call 911 and use [glucagon](/en/emergencies/glucagon/) if there is some.
:::

Knowing where the kit is matters more than knowing the chart. Ask the person you're with where they keep their sugar.
