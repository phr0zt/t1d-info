For families: the sentences that do damage

Never

  • “You’ll go blind / lose a foot / end up on dialysis”
  • “My grandmother had diabetes and she...”
  • “Is your sugar good today?”
  • “Should you be eating that?”
  • “You just need more discipline”
  • “What did you do?” after a high reading

Instead

  • “How are you doing with it?”
  • “What would actually help right now?”
  • “That looks like a lot of work.”
  • “Tell me a bit more about what worries you.”
  • Or nothing at all about the number on the screen.

Worth knowing

Type 1 is autoimmune. Nobody caused it — not diet, not parenting, not sugar.

Judgement causes concealment. People under-report readings they expect to be judged for, and hidden numbers are a safety problem.

The help that is wanted is usually quiet: learning glucagon, knowing sick-day rules, driving to appointments, not making a scene at dinner.

Across international surveys, 96% of people with diabetes find “sufferer” unacceptable and 83% reject “good” and “bad” applied to blood sugar. Sources: dStigmatize · ADA 2017 · Diabetes Canada Language Matters · Diabetes Australia.

The left column is drawn verbatim from published avoid-lists, not from opinion. Say so — it stops the slide being heard as a personal rebuke.
