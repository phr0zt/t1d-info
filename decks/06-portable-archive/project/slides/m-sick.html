Sick days: the insulin does not stop

Illness raises your insulin needs even when you are eating nothing at all. Stopping insulin because no food is going in is the single most common route into ketoacidosis.

Keeps going

  • Your long-acting insulin, always
  • Checks every two to four hours, overnight too
  • Ketone checks, whatever the glucose says
  • Fluids, sipped steadily

Usually pauses

While you are dehydrated: SGLT2 inhibitors, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, diuretics, metformin, and anti-inflammatories like ibuprofen. They strain the kidneys when you are dry.

Get your own hold list from your pharmacist, on paper, before you are ever ill enough to need it.

Carbs in liquid form

If solids won’t stay down: regular pop, juice, sports drink, ice pops, sweet tea. Plus something salty — broth or a rehydration solution.

The box, ready in advance

In-date ketone strips, fast sugar, sugary and salty fluids, a thermometer, your team’s after-hours number, and a written plan. Sick judgement is bad judgement.

A pump failing is its own sick day: with no long-acting depot, ketones climb fast. Source: Canadian sick-day medication guidance.

The written plan matters because the person reading it may be a parent or partner while the patient is too unwell to think.
